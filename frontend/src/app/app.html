WHY UNDEREMPLOYMENT MATTERS

Graduation is supposed to mark the beginning of a career, but for many students it begins with uncertainty. Across the United States, college graduates may struggle to find jobs that match their education and skills. In addition to unemployment, underemployment has become a growing concern as the market has become saturated with young educated professionals.

Underemployment refers to people working jobs that underutilize their skills and education. This game simulates the choices of college students, using our prediction model to calculate your likelihood of finding work that uses your education and skills by graduation.

Behind the scenes: how this score is calculated

Your first-job chances are (1 - chance of unemployment) * (1 - chance of underemployment). Both chances come from our gradient-boosted model trained on 296,685 US graduates, averaged over the real mix of graduates by gender, race and birthplace, so only your choices move the score.

An internship multiplies the odds of underemployment by {{ research.internshipOddsRatio }} (Strada & Burning Glass Institute, 2024). Ten-year chances: first-job chances * {{ research.stayCollegeLevel * 100 }}% + the rest * {{ research.leaveUnderemployment * 100 }}%, from the same study. Choices with no measured effect, like the elective and the AI certificate, show a fact but do not change the score.

These are group statistics, not predictions about you. Graduates who made these choices may differ in ways the data does not capture.

BEFORE AND AFTER AI: YOUTH UNEMPLOYMENT AND NEET IN THE US

ChatGPT launched in November 2022, and generative AI spread quickly after. Comparing the years before and after gives a first look at how the youth job market has shifted in the AI era.

Before AI (grey band, 2022): youth unemployment and NEET were steady or falling. After AI (2023-2025): youth unemployment has risen every year, from 7.9% in 2023 to 10.0% in 2025, and the share of young people who are NEET has edged up.

Here the grey band marks the after-AI years, 2023-2025. Both lines rise, but at different slopes: youth unemployment went from 7.9% to 10.0% (+2.0 percentage points), while overall unemployment went from 3.6% to 4.3% (+0.6 percentage points). The wider economy did cool somewhat, but youth unemployment rose roughly three times as steeply. Young people are now 2.33 times as likely to be unemployed as the labor force overall, the widest gap since at least 2018. The timing lines up with the advent of AI, though this data alone cannot show that AI caused it.

All of this comes from the World Bank's World Development Indicators, the dataset this challenge targets.

BREAKING DOWN THE NEET POPULATION
  • Unemployment: youth unemployment rate * labor force participation rate. This is the share of the youth population that is unemployed.
  • Inactivity: NEET - unemployment. This is the portion of NEET who are not looking for work.
  1. The 2020 spike was due to unemployment, which rose from 4.69% to 8.04%, while inactivity stayed nearly unchanged.
  2. NEET stayed higher in 2021-2023 than in 2019 because of inactivity. By 2022-2023, unemployment was back below its 2019 level, but inactivity stayed elevated, rising from 5.7% to 6.7%: more young people were not looking for work at all.
  3. The 2023-2025 increase was again due to unemployment, which rose from 4.47% to 5.55%.

Behind the scenes: how the model works

The odds above come from a gradient-boosted decision tree classifier, the same family of models as XGBoost. It builds small decision trees one after another (118 to 242 per outcome, stopping once new trees no longer improve accuracy). Each tree asks a few yes-or-no questions about a graduate, such as whether they studied engineering or live in California, and corrects the errors of the trees before it. Together, the trees produce a probability for each outcome.

Gradient boosting is one of the most widely used methods for data organized in rows and columns, like survey records, where it routinely matches or beats more complex approaches. We trained one model per outcome on 296,685 graduates and tested it on households it had never seen. Its estimates are well calibrated: when it predicts 10%, about 10% of those graduates actually had that outcome. As a check, we also trained a simpler logistic regression; the two models scored within 0.01 AUC of each other, so the results do not depend on the choice of model.

Measured on households held out from training (about 1 in 5). AUC is the chance the model ranks a graduate who had the outcome above one who did not: 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect. Calibration error is the average gap between predicted and actual rates. We do not report plain accuracy: with only 5% of graduates unemployed, a model that always answers "employed" would be 95% accurate and useless. Background characteristics explain part of the picture, not all of it, which is why the model gives odds rather than verdicts.

DOES A DEGREE PROTECT YOU?

A university degree lowers unemployment in most of the region, but not all of it. In 10 of 30 countries, graduates are more likely to be unemployed than the workforce as a whole. Haiti has the widest gap, at 11.9 percentage points.
